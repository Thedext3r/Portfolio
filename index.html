Problem

Neighbors need items they don't own — drills, ladders, camping gear — but have no trustworthy, low-friction way to borrow them.

What I Did

Surveyed 28 residents, synthesized six user archetypes, and designed an end-to-end borrowing flow anchored by a trust score.

Outcome

A complete flow — RWA-verified onboarding, browse, request, lender review, pickup, return, and rating — grounded in survey-validated demand.

The Problem

The real blocker was awkwardness, not distrust.

Neighbors need each other's occasionally-used items, but have no trustworthy way to discover and borrow them — so they default to buying, renting, or going without. Social awkwardness was the single most-cited hesitation, ahead of concerns about item condition or reliability.

"People weren't primarily afraid of being cheated — they were afraid of the social discomfort of requesting a favor. That single insight reframed everything downstream."

Current Workaround 1

Buy it immediately on Amazon, even for one-time use.

Current Workaround 2

Unstructured WhatsApp group posts with zero accountability.

Blocker #1

Not knowing neighbors well enough to ask unprompted.

Trust Driver #1

Verified identity + flat number, ranked far above financial incentives.

User Research

Three findings that shaped everything.

Structured survey across four sections — demographics, borrowing behavior, pain points, and app expectations — paired with a competitor scan of Peerby, Fat Llama, Nextdoor, and WhatsApp groups.

01

Borrowing is common but undocumented

Most respondents had borrowed from a neighbor at least once, yet almost none used anything more structured than asking in person or a WhatsApp post.

02

Trust outweighs price

A verified identity, a lender's real flat number, and past exchange history mattered more to respondents than any financial incentive to lend — deposits ranked below social proof.

03

Closed beats open

Respondents were consistently more comfortable with an app limited to verified residents of their own society than with an open, distance-based map of nearby strangers. Strongest single signal in the study.

Constraint I want to name directly

This is a 28-response survey from my own network — a meaningfully smaller sample than a shipped product would need, and it measures stated intent, not observed behavior. The honest read is that Borrow's concept is validated; its actual screens are not — yet.

User Personas

Six residents. Six relationships with borrowing.

Synthesized from 28 survey responses. Each archetype drove at least one feature decision — the Social Avoider alone shaped the entire request-flow structure.

01 · Pragmatic Borrower Primary

"I just need a drill for one weekend — why buy it?"

One-off tasks Values speed Low friction

Core Need: Fast, searchable item discovery within their society.
Design Impact: Drove the browse-first Home screen and category filters.

02 · Social Avoider Key Insight

"I'd rather just buy it than knock on a stranger's door."

Avoids direct asks High social friction App-first

Core Need: Async, impersonal request flow — no cold messaging.
Design Impact: Structured request form instead of open chat.

03 · Generous Connector Primary

"I have so much stuff collecting dust — happy to lend."

Proactive lender Goodwill motivated Community focus

Core Need: Easy listing, visibility of who's borrowing and why.
Design Impact: Justified trust-score-first over deposit-first model.

04 · Reluctant Lender Secondary

"What if they damage it? I need some kind of protection."

High-value items Checks history Safety net

Core Need: Visible trust score + optional deposit field per listing.
Design Impact: Added optional deposit; prominent trust score on requests.

05 · Indifferent Maximiser Secondary

"I'd try it if there's clearly something in it for me."

Low intrinsic drive Social proof Needs nudges

Core Need: Weekly digest of nearby items and requests.
Design Impact: Flagged digest notifications as next-build feature post-MVP.

06 · New Resident Edge Case

"I just moved in — I don't know anyone here yet."

Zero connections Needs trust cues First-timer

Core Need: Onboarding explaining closed-society trust model.
Design Impact: Strengthened RWA and flat-verified onboarding screens.

Design Decisions

Three decisions, each with a trade-off.

Click each card to expand and review what was considered, what was chosen, and what was traded away.

Considered A Nextdoor-style open map showing all nearby users within a radius — maximizes item supply.
Chose A closed, RWA-verified single-society scope. Onboarding requires selecting a verified society and flat number.
Why The two top-cited blockers were not knowing neighbors and lack of verification. Respondents were consistently more comfortable with a verified, closed community.
Trade-off Item availability is lower in smaller societies. The model requires dense housing clusters to achieve critical mass.
Considered A mandatory deposit/escrow model (Fat Llama style), providing financial guarantees for cautious lenders.
Chose A visible trust score built from profile verification, exchange count, and on-time-return rate — shown on every request.
Why Survey dominant trust driver was verified identity, not cash. Mandatory deposits create heavy checkout drop-off.
Trade-off Lenders of higher-value items lack full financial insurance (mitigated via optional deposit listing field).
Considered A lightweight, always-on chat as primary coordination — simplest to build and closest to WhatsApp.
Chose A structured request form (dates, message, pickup preference) that a lender explicitly accepts or declines. In-app chat unlocks only after acceptance.
Why The Social Avoider persona needed to never message a stranger unprompted. Declining a structured card feels far less confrontational than saying no in open chat.
Trade-off Coordinating partial-day adjustments or edge cases is slower than direct chat.

Reflection

What I'd do differently.

Close the research-testing gap

I validated the concept with a survey before design, but never took the finished screens back to real residents. Next time: budget 3–5 moderated usability sessions on the clickable Figma prototype.

What I'd build next

The Society Directory and weekly-digest notification for the 'indifferent maximizer' segment. And an algorithm for trust score portability across multiple housing societies.

The lesson carried forward

A single well-evidenced insight — that awkwardness, not distrust, was the real blocker — did more to shape a good product than any amount of feature brainstorming. Narrowing scope (one society, not an open map) is often a stronger trust-building move than adding more trust features.
